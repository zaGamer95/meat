    Grading

    At a Korean counter you will see 한우 beside US, Australian and New Zealand beef, each carrying a grade from its own country. Those grades cannot be converted into one another, because they are not measuring the same thing.

    What differs is the question each system asks. Korea and Japan ask how much fat is inside the muscle. The US asks that too, plus the age of the animal. Australia asks something else entirely — how will this particular cut eat, cooked this particular way. New Zealand mostly does not ask at all. A 1++ and an A5 and a MSA 5★ are three different claims, not three points on one ladder.
    What each system actually measures.
    System What you see What it measures What it will not tell you
    Korea — KAPE 1++ / 1+ / 1 / 2 / 3
    plus A / B / C    		 Marbling, meat colour, fat colour and texture assessed separately — the lowest sets the grade. The letter is carcass yield. How tender it will actually eat, or how it was raised.
    United States — USDA Prime / Choice / Select Marbling, read against the animal’s maturity. Breed, feed, or anything about a specific cut.
    Japan — JMGA A5, A4, B3 … Letter = yield. Number = the worst of marbling, meat colour, firmness and fat colour. How rich it is within a grade — A5 covers a wide band.
    Australia — MSA 3★ / 4★ / 5★ Predicted eating quality, scored per cut and per cooking method. Marbling as a headline number — that is a separate AUS-MEAT score.
    New Zealand usually none Carcass classification — sex, age, fat depth, muscling. Not eating quality. Almost anything a shopper wants. You are buying the brand and the spec.

    Korea 한우, 육우, 젖소 — the label before the grade

    Before any grade, Korean beef is labelled by what the animal was. Three categories, defined by law, and they are not a quality ranking — they are a statement about the breed and the animal’s history.

    식육의 종류 — the category printed above the grade.
    Label What the animal was What to expect
    한우 The Korean native breed. Fine marbling, long feeding, the highest prices. Most 1++ beef is 한우.
    육우 Beef breeds and crossbreds, Holstein males, dairy females that never calved — and imported live cattle raised in Korea for six months or more. Leaner, cheaper, rarely above 2등급. Dairy-breed genetics do not marble like 한우.
    젖소 Dairy cows that have calved. Older animals. Firm, lean, deeply flavoured — a stewing and broth animal.
    국내산 does not mean 한우. Origin is printed separately from breed, as 국내산(한우), 국내산(육우) or 외국산(미국, 육우). Because live cattle imported and then raised in Korea for six months qualify as 국내산, an Australian-born animal finished locally can legitimately be sold as 국내산(육우). Read the word in the brackets, not the one in front of it.

    You may hear 식용우 used loosely for “cattle raised to eat.” It is not one of the three label categories above, so it will not appear on a price tag — 육우 is the term you are looking for.

    Korea The KAPE grade: 1++ down to 3

    Korean beef carries two grades at once, printed together as something like 1++ A. They are unrelated to each other, and only the first one concerns your dinner.

    육질등급 and the marbling score behind it. Standards in force since 1 December 2019.
    육질등급 근내지방도 (BMS) Before Dec 2019 In practice
    1++ No. 7–9 No. 8–9 Intensely rich. Roughly 15.6% intramuscular fat and up.
    1+ No. 6 No. 6–7 About 12.3–15.6% fat. For many people the better everyday buy.
    1 No. 4–5 No. 4–5 Clearly marbled, still very good on a grill.
    2 No. 2–3 No. 2–3 Lean. Where most 육우 lands. Better braised than grilled.
    3 No. 1 No. 1 Very lean. Stew, soup, 국거리.
    The 2019 revision made 1++ easier to reach, not harder. The threshold dropped from BMS No. 8 to No. 7 — from about 17% intramuscular fat to about 15.6% — as a deliberate step away from rewarding marbling above everything else. A 1++ label from before December 2019 and one from after are not the same claim.
    How it is judged
    Since the same revision, marbling, meat colour, fat colour and texture are each assessed separately and the lowest of them sets the final grade — the same principle Japan uses. Marbling no longer simply overrides the rest.
    The number in brackets
    1++ and 1+ are now shown with the marbling score alongside, so 1++(9) is a meaningfully fattier animal than 1++(7) at the same grade. If a shop will not show you the number, assume the bottom of the band.
    Yield
    육량등급 — A B C, calculated from backfat thickness, ribeye area and carcass weight. It describes how much saleable meat came off the animal, which matters to the butcher’s margin and not to you. A 1++ C is still 1++ beef.
    Watch for
    Imported beef is not given a Korean grade. A US or Australian grade on a package is that country’s system, not a KAPE equivalent.

    United States USDA quality grades

    Grading is voluntary and paid for by the packer, so ungraded American beef is not failed beef — it is often simply unassessed.

    Scale
    Prime Choice Select for anything you will meet at retail, with lower grades going to processing.
    Basis
    Marbling in the ribeye at the twelfth rib, judged against maturity — a young animal needs less marbling for the same grade than an older one.
    Scarcity
    Prime is roughly one carcass in ten, though that share has climbed sharply over the last decade as feeding practices changed.
    Watch for
    Brand names like Certified Angus Beef are private specifications stacked on top of a USDA grade, not a higher grade. They usually mean upper Choice with tighter marbling requirements.

    Japan JMGA — the A5 you have heard of

    Letter
    Yield only. A is an above-standard meat yield, B standard, C below. It says nothing about how the beef tastes. A B5 can eat better than an A4.
    Number
    5 down to 1, set by four separate assessments — marbling on a 1–12 BMS scale, meat colour and brightness, firmness and texture, and fat colour and quality. The final number is the lowest of the four, so one weak category caps the whole carcass.
    The catch
    Grade 5 covers BMS 8 through 12. That is an enormous range — the top of A5 is dramatically richer than the bottom of it, at a very different price. Shops selling serious wagyu will quote the BMS number; if nobody will, assume the low end.

    Australia MSA — a different question entirely

    Meat Standards Australia was built backwards from consumer taste panels rather than forwards from carcass appearance. It is the only major system that grades a cut cooked a particular way instead of a whole animal.

    Output
    3★ good everyday, 4★ better than everyday, 5★ premium — assigned per cut, per method. The same carcass can yield a 5★ roast and a 3★ grilling steak.
    Inputs
    Marbling, ossification as a proxy for maturity, pH, hang method, hump height as an indicator of Bos indicus content, and the ageing period.
    Separately
    Australia also runs an AUS-MEAT marbling score of 0–9, and Australian Wagyu is usually sold on that number rather than on stars.
    Why it matters
    MSA is the system that will tell you a lean grass-fed cut is excellent. The marbling-first systems structurally cannot.

    New Zealand classification, not grading

    Reality
    There is no consumer-facing quality grade comparable to the others. Carcasses are classified by sex, dentition-estimated age, fat depth and muscling — a description of the animal, not a prediction of the meal.
    What to read instead
    The brand, the breed, and the finishing claim. Almost all New Zealand beef is pasture-raised to slaughter, and that single fact tells you more about how it will cook than any number would.

    Breeds

    Grades describe one carcass. Breed and feeding describe what you are broadly buying — and for a shopper, the differences come down to three practical things: how rich it is, how it behaves in the pan, and how much of it you should put on a plate.

    Grass-fed NZ · AU pasture
    Black Angus US · AU grain-finished
    한우 Hanwoo Korea
    和牛 Wagyu Japan
    Leaner · firmer · eat more Richer · softer · eat less

    Korea 한우 Hanwoo

    Eats like
    Fine, evenly dispersed marbling rather than thick seams, and a clean beef flavour that is less overtly sweet than wagyu. Fed long — typically around thirty months — which is a large part of why it costs what it does.
    In the pan
    The fat softens at low heat, so thin slices on a hot grill are its best form. Thick steaks work but waste the quality; the marbling is doing its job in seconds, not minutes.
    Portion
    At 1++, treat 150 g per person as generous. Richness arrives faster than hunger leaves.
    Buy it when
    You want 구이 — 등심, 채끝 or 갈비살 over charcoal, salt only, nothing in the way.

    Japan 和牛 Wagyu

    Eats like
    The extreme end. Intramuscular fat that is high in monounsaturated fat and melts near body temperature, which is the literal mechanism behind “melts in the mouth.” At the top of A5 the texture is closer to a rich sauce than to steak.
    In the pan
    Very hot, very briefly, no added fat — it renders its own immediately. Past medium rare the fat leaves the muscle and you are paying for something that went into the pan.
    Portion
    80–120 g per person for A5. A full steak-sized serving is genuinely unpleasant for most people, and that is not a failure of appetite.
    Watch for
    “Wagyu” outside Japan usually means a crossbreed, most often Wagyu over Angus. That is a legitimate and often excellent product, but it is a different thing from Japanese purebred, and it should not be priced the same.

    US · Australia Black Angus

    Eats like
    The most assertively beefy of the four. Marbling in visible flecks and seams rather than a fine web, firmer fat, and a flavour that stands up to sauce, smoke and char instead of being drowned by them.
    In the pan
    The most forgiving. Enough fat to survive a minute too long, enough structure to hold a hard crust. This is the breed to learn on.
    Portion
    200–250 g is a normal steak and stays enjoyable to the last bite.
    Watch for
    Grain-finished Angus and grass-finished Angus eat very differently. The breed on the label tells you less than the finishing does.

    New Zealand · Australia Grass-fed

    Eats like
    Leaner, firmer, more mineral — some people read it as gamey, others as simply more like beef. The fat is faintly yellow from beta-carotene in pasture, which is a sign of diet, not of age or poor quality.
    In the pan
    Cooks noticeably faster, because there is less intramuscular fat to heat through. Treating it like grain-fed beef is the usual mistake — pull it earlier than instinct says, and rest it longer.
    Portion
    250 g and up. It is the one you can eat a proper plateful of.
    Buy it when
    You want beef flavour rather than fat texture, or you are cooking something where rendered fat would be in the way — tataki, roast beef served cold, salads.
    One rule that travels across all of it. Richness and portion size move in opposite directions. The systems above are mostly measuring how much fat is in the muscle, and the more there is, the less of it you want on the plate. A 5★ grass-fed sirloin and a 1++ 한우 등심 can both be excellent dinners — they are just not the same size dinner.
